# Write-up — Author Support & Communication Portal

**Approach.** I read the brief as two products sharing one core: an author who is anxious about money and timelines and needs a straight answer fast, and a small ops team living in a queue all day. That shaped everything. The ticket is the core object; books and royalties are read-only context that makes tickets answerable. I separated the login identity (`User`) from the dataset's author record (`Author`), so access control comes from the JWT and never from ids the client supplies. The stack (React + TypeScript client, Express + TypeScript API, Prisma over SQLite locally and PostgreSQL in production) is deliberately boring: every choice is one a BookLeaf maintainer can pick up without me in the room.

**Priorities.** First, the complete author loop on the real dataset — books with every field shown honestly (the two in-production books have null MRP and royalty rates in BookLeaf's own data; the UI says so instead of inventing numbers), query submission, and a thread the author can watch update live. Second, the admin loop the evaluation actually weighs: a filterable queue sorted urgent-first and oldest-waiting, overrides that are recorded as human decisions rather than silent edits, internal notes that are structurally invisible to authors (proven by a test, not a promise), and drafts a human always edits before sending. Third, the API discipline — one error shape, validation on every input, 34 automated tests.

**The AI layer.** I did not pick models by reputation. I ran a bake-off on the brief's own seven sample queries and a draft built from a real author's figures, across three providers and four models. gpt-4o-mini won classification (tied-best accuracy, cleanest JSON, about $0.00004 a ticket); Claude Haiku 4.5 won drafts (best tone, and the only draft that asked the author to confirm her bank details, as the brief's own model answer does). Gemini Flash was rejected on evidence: its reasoning tokens ate the budget and returned stubs at twenty times the cost. Two design rules matter more than the model pick. Grounding lives in one Knowledge Base module shared by the LLM prompts and the fallback engine, so policy can never drift between paths. And every AI call fails closed: a deterministic rules engine behind the same interface scores 7/7 on the brief's samples, so an outage, a rate limit, or a missing key degrades the portal to rules-based triage instead of breaking it. Drafts are generated once and cached per ticket, which keeps the AI bill in single-digit dollars a month at BookLeaf's stated scale.

**Trade-offs.** SSE with a polling baseline instead of WebSockets — simpler, proxy-friendly, and the "real-time" requirement is met with a resilient floor. Attachments are UI-only, as the brief permits; object storage is the first production addition. The event hub is in-process; Redis pub/sub is the documented swap when the API scales past one instance.

**In production**, this becomes: Postgres with migrations, Redis-backed events, attachment storage with scanning, SLA timers and breach alerts on the queue, few-shot classification for the one ambiguous case the bake-off found, and per-model cost dashboards from the usage the server already logs. What is here is the honest skeleton of that system, with the parts authors and admins touch every day finished properly.
